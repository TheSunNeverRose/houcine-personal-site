---
title: Persuasion Isn't Negotiation
description: A distinction that matters when the goal is an agreement rather than a change of mind.
date: 2026-09-22
updated: 2026-10-16
category: Negotiation

I keep coming back to a basic difference: changing someone's mind is not the same job as agreeing what to do next.

Imagine a contractor and a client arguing over a Friday deadline. The client keeps saying the date was promised; the contractor keeps explaining why the remaining work needs another day. They are trying to win different versions of the same argument. But the decision in front of them might be narrower: what can be delivered on Friday, what moves to Monday, and whether the changed schedule affects the price.

That example is hypothetical, but it makes the distinction easier to see. Persuasion tries to change a belief or preference. Negotiation tries to settle terms when people have different priorities. Sometimes you need both. Sometimes you can agree on a workable arrangement while still disagreeing about who was right.

One part I find easy to overlook is that a demand may hide a constraint. A deadline might stand in for another commitment. A price may reflect a budget limit rather than a judgement about quality. Those are possible explanations, not facts about the other person. Asking is safer than assuming you know what they mean.

The practical question is what can actually change. Maybe the deadline cannot move, but the scope can. Maybe the price is fixed but support, timing, or payment terms are not. If nothing can change, then the honest question is whether there is any agreement available at all.

I have not documented a real negotiation using a formal method, so I won't claim this is a tested framework. For me, the distinction is useful as a way to read a disagreement: are we trying to change the other person's view, or do we need to make a decision together? I don't need to turn every disagreement into an argument to win.
