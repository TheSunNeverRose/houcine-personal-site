## The idea

This is a hypothetical local service concept for practising the early stages of business design. It is not an operating business, and no customers, sales, or market validation are claimed.

The point of the exercise is to make the assumptions visible before becoming attached to the idea.

## What needs to be clear first

- **Customer:** who would use the service, and in what specific situation?
- **Problem:** what do they do today, and why is that not good enough?
- **Offer:** what exactly would they receive, and what is outside the scope?
- **Acquisition:** how would the first customers hear about it?
- **Delivery:** what time, equipment, skills, and ongoing costs would be required?

If these cannot be described clearly, the financial model will only give a false sense of precision.

## The basic economics

For a simple service, the contribution per sale can be approximated as:

`contribution per sale = price charged − variable cost per sale`

If the contribution is positive, a simplified break-even estimate is:

`break-even sales = fixed costs ÷ contribution per sale`

This leaves out important complications such as taxes, owner compensation, changing demand, refunds, seasonality, and capacity limits. A real model would need to account for them.

## Assumptions versus evidence

Every number in a future model should be labelled as one of three things:

- **Verified:** supported by a reliable source or observed transaction.
- **Estimate:** a reasoned approximation with its method recorded.
- **Hypothesis:** something that still needs to be tested.

The distinction matters more than the apparent precision of a spreadsheet.

## What would justify stopping?

If likely customers do not care enough to try the service, if reaching them costs too much, or if realistic delivery costs leave no sustainable margin, the concept may not deserve further investment. It could be revised, narrowed, or abandoned.

## Next step

Choose a specific service and customer segment, speak to potential customers without leading their answers, gather realistic cost estimates, and build a simple spreadsheet. Until then, this remains a practice exercise, not a business plan supported by market evidence.
